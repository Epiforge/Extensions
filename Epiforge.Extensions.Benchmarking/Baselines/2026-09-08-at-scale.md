# At scale

*2026-09-08 — `ScaleComparisonBenchmarks`, DynamicData 9.4.33, DefaultJob, MemoryDiagnoser, 1,000 / 10,000 / 100,000 elements*

## What is being asked

The same filtered view as `DynamicDataComparisonBenchmarks`, at three sizes, to find out which of its two answers is a property of the design and which was a property of a thousand elements. Only DynamicData's cache is measured; its list path costs 210x its own cache at a thousand elements and would have measured nothing else at a hundred thousand.

| arm | 1,000 | 10,000 | 100,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ChangeEveryRankUnobserved` | 9.416 μs / 46.88 KB | 88.587 μs / 468.75 KB | 1,062.750 μs / 4,687.5 KB |
| `ChangeEveryRankWithExpressions` | 16.255 μs / **46.88 KB** | 180.673 μs / **468.75 KB** | 6,567.369 μs / **4,687.5 KB** |
| `ChangeEveryRankWithDynamicDataCache` | 200.076 μs / 640.81 KB | 2,213.867 μs / 6,406.44 KB | 41,747.151 μs / 64,075.95 KB |
| `ConstructAndDisposeWithExpressions` | 291.333 μs / 965.19 KB | 13,287.862 μs / 9,472.79 KB | 163,093.662 μs / 93,434.11 KB |
| `ConstructAndDisposeWithDynamicDataCache` | 2,276.632 μs / 4,119.34 KB | 45,621.865 μs / 41,126.76 KB | 720,098.899 μs / 406,498.33 KB |

## The allocation answer is exact, and it holds

**This library's propagation allocation ratio is 1.00 at every size.** Not "close to the floor" — the same number the unobserved arm reports, to the byte, at a thousand, ten thousand and a hundred thousand elements. A hundred thousand property changes through a live filtered view allocate nothing that the changes themselves did not already allocate.

**DynamicData's cache is 13.67x the floor at every size**, also to two decimal places. That is 608 bytes per change at every scale, exactly as at a thousand. Both libraries' allocation behavior is a constant per change; one of the constants is zero.

Per-element construction is linear for both. This library: 988, 970, 957 bytes per element. DynamicData: 4,218, 4,211, 4,163. **The 4.3x construction gap is a property of the design and not of the size**, and this library's per-element construction floor — 983.9 B/element measured directly this morning — is confirmed by a completely different instrument at a hundred times the scale.

## The time answer is not flat, and the degradation is ours

Subtracting the unobserved floor and dividing by the change count:

| | 1,000 | 10,000 | 100,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| this library, per change | 6.8 ns | 9.2 ns | **55.0 ns** |
| DynamicData's cache, per change | 190.7 ns | 212.5 ns | 406.8 ns |
| ratio | 27.9x | 23.1x | **7.4x** |

**This library's per-change time degrades 8x between ten thousand and a hundred thousand elements; DynamicData's degrades 1.9x.** The lead narrows from 27.9x to 7.4x, and it narrows because of something happening here rather than something improving there.

The cause is almost certainly the memory hierarchy. A hundred thousand observation objects, each holding a subscription and an evaluation, do not fit in cache; every property change then touches cold memory. DynamicData already pays enough per change — 608 bytes of change set, a stream of operators — that a cache miss is a smaller fraction of what it was already spending. **A library which has driven its own per-change work to near zero has nothing left to hide a cache miss behind.** That is the honest reading, and it is the flip side of the allocation result rather than a contradiction of it.

**This was not predicted.** The instrument's own remarks anticipated the construction allocation at a hundred thousand — "hundreds of megabytes per operation in at least one of the arms," and it was 406 MB — but nothing here anticipated that propagation time would stop being flat. The allocation figure was predicted and held; the timing figure was not looked for and did not hold.

## What this means for a reader

Still 7.4x faster per change and still allocating nothing, at a hundred thousand elements. But **the honest sentence is not "the advantage grows with scale" — it shrinks.** It shrinks from very large to large. A README should say the propagation advantage is largest at the sizes most applications actually use, and that at six figures both libraries are dominated by memory traffic.

Construction is where a reader at scale should look hardest: 163 ms and 93 MB to stand up a view over a hundred thousand elements is not free, and it is the number that decides whether this library suits a workload which builds and tears down views over very large collections. It is still 4.4x better than the alternative measured.

## What has not been measured

Whether the per-change degradation is cache or something else — a cheap test would be a hundred thousand elements with only a thousand observed. Whether construction can be made to scale better than it does; both libraries are superlinear in construction time and neither was investigated. Retention, which is the next instrument.
